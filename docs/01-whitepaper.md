# Epistrel — A Viewpoint-Aware Memory Engine for Character/Actor GenAI

**Whitepaper · Working draft**

> **Epistrel** = *Epistemic* + *Relationship*: a memory engine built around what each character knows and believes, and how characters stand in relation to one another and the truth.

---

## Abstract

Epistrel is an open-source memory engine for character- and actor-based generative AI: long-running roleplay, interactive fiction, multi-agent simulations, NPC dialogue, and real games. It exists to solve the problem that defines the category and that no current product solves well — **keeping a coherent, long-lived world when the only thing with memory is a language model whose memory is small, expensive, and unreliable.**

The central move is to stop treating the chat transcript as the world. In Epistrel, the authoritative world lives in a transactional, event-sourced store; a language model **proposes**: it reads a selectively assembled slice of that world and writes prose plus structured proposals back, and the engine decides what is accepted. On that foundation Epistrel adds the thing generic agent-memory libraries lack and that character fiction specifically requires: a **per-character epistemic model** — each character has their own partial, time-bound, possibly-false view of the world — plus a **validation layer** that checks every claim it detects in rendered output against canon and rules before it reaches the player, and a **commitment lifecycle** that decides exactly when a hidden truth becomes fixed fact.

Epistrel is delivered as a complete engine: the consumer sends a small request and receives a finished message — checked against every claim the engine detects in it, to the extent the story's engine profile enables checking (§4.6; Lite does no structured claim checking at all). All of the hard work — retrieval, verification, grading, re-generation, commitment, guardrails, repair — happens inside Epistrel.

This document explains how the system works, the concrete problems it solves, and the mechanism that solves each.

---

## 1. The problem

Current character-AI and roleplay systems share one architecture: a character definition, a premise, and then one ever-growing chat log, sometimes with a little retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) bolted on. This fails in predictable ways:

1. **Memory collapse.** The world's entire history must fit in the context window. As the game grows, it can't. Summarization distills the world into something small enough to fit, destroying detail; static "lorebooks" help but are hand-maintained. Long games degrade.
2. **Naive retrieval misses the interesting queries.** Vector RAG matches surface semantics. It handles "what did you think of Bob?" but fails on "remember those guys from the game a month ago?" — pronouns carry no embedding signal, time is a filter not a meaning, and abstract questions don't sit near concrete memories.
3. **No knowledge asymmetry.** Every character effectively reads the same context, so nobody can hold a secret, a false belief, or private knowledge. Dramatic irony, mysteries, and intrigue cannot be sustained. This is the category's largest expressive limitation.
4. **The model is the world's only authority — and it's unreliable.** It contradicts established facts, fabricates against canon, and breaks world or safety rules, with no external truth to check against, so the errors reach the player.
5. **Nothing is decided until it's said — so nothing is stable.** When a character's phone buzzes and three turns later reveals "it was just a meme," the model never decided what the message was when it buzzed; it invents a convenient answer at reveal time, and regeneration produces a different answer each time. The world has no fixed past.
6. **Retroactive edits corrupt silently.** When a user directs "actually, Bobby went to UCLA," everything that depended on "Ohio State" is now wrong, and nothing reconciles it.

Epistrel is built to solve all six — and to extend cleanly to real games whose state lives partly outside the engine.

---

## 2. Thesis

> **The model proposes; the engine validates. The world is authoritative, external, event-sourced state. In the engine profiles that enable them, each character is given only their own viewpoint of it, every claim the engine detects in a rendered output is checked against that state — and against external authorities the game owns — before it is accepted, and every consequential truth is committed the moment something depends on it. The lightest profile, Lite, runs none of that machinery and says so (§4.6).**

Everything below follows from that sentence.

---

## 3. Foundational principles

- **The model proposes; the engine validates.** A model turns world-state into prose and proposed state-changes. It is never the source of truth — but it is not merely a typist either: finding the claims in its own prose, judging salience, estimating elapsed time, settling fuzzy entailment, and parts of belief adjudication and repair also use a model. What the engine guarantees, and what it measures, is spelled out in §10.
- **Authoritative external state.** Facts, events, beliefs, and rules live in Epistrel. The transcript is an output artifact, not the database.
- **Event sourcing.** The log of what happened is the truth; current-state tables are derived projections, rebuildable at any time — yielding save/load, rewind, branching, and audit almost for free.
- **Per-character epistemic viewpoint.** Memory is partitioned by *knower*. What is objectively true, what a character knows, what they believe (including falsely), and what they believe about others are distinct and separately queryable. **This is Epistrel's reason to exist.**
- **Epistrel does the work; the consumer is thin.** Epistrel comprises two internal components — the **Engine Core** (deterministic data layer) and the **Orchestrator** (the agent that does the work). The consumer sends a small request and receives a finished message, checked according to the story's engine profile; it implements no memory logic.
- **Resolve before you retrieve.** Vague, referential, or temporal queries are resolved into concrete entities, time windows, and intent *before* vector search runs. Retrieval is a pipeline, not a single lookup.
- **Deterministic-first, cognition-on-the-residual.** The Engine Core is deterministic and makes no model calls. The Orchestrator reaches for a model only for the irreducibly fuzzy residual, and as little as possible, to minimize cost, latency, and nondeterminism.
- **Eager-but-lazy commitment.** A hidden truth is committed the instant any character's thoughts or actions depend on its value, or narration makes it salient (§8) — never forced earlier (which over-constrains and kills regeneration) and never later (which permits retcon).
- **Validate, then repair — least-invasively.** Rendered output is checked against canon and rules; violations are fixed with the cheapest repair that resolves them, from trimming a clause to regenerating a span under corrected constraints.
- **Policy never yields to memory.** Safety and world invariants live in an immutable layer that memory — which can be poisoned — can never override.
- **Tracked values have an owner, and owners decide objective truth — not knowledge.** Simple game values (gold, health, flags) live in Epistrel as **States** that rewind and fork with the story; values a game must own itself come from external providers. Either way the owner fixes what is objectively true, while characters still only know what they have a way to know.
- **A story is a self-contained dataset.** No data references another story, which makes forking a full independent copy and deletion a trivial, side-effect-free scoped delete.
- **The consumer chooses on safety-vs-smoothness tradeoffs.** Where a behavior trades continuity safety against UX smoothness (e.g., how history-rewrite conflicts are resolved), Epistrel exposes the policy and lets the consumer pick, defaulting to the safe end.

## 4. Architecture at a glance

Epistrel is one product with two internal components and a deliberately thin consumer.

**Epistrel = Engine Core + Orchestrator.**

- **Engine Core** — the deterministic data layer. Owns the event store, projections, the epistemic model and visibility scoping, retrieval mechanics and the resolution pipeline, the *deterministic* verification check (set logic over normalized canon), the commitment lifecycle and promissory ledger, the immutable policy store, and memory-mutation primitives. **Makes no model calls**; identical inputs produce identical outputs.
- **Orchestrator** — the agent that does the work. Owns the turn loop: retrieve (via Engine Core) → render (model) → verify (Engine Core deterministic check, escalating to a model only on the fuzzy residual) → grade → re-generate/repair → polish → return. Makes all model calls through a per-purpose **Model Access Layer**. Is **extensible** via tools/Skills/MCP so it can reach external data the game owns. Background jobs (consolidation, importance, salience, extraction) run in Orchestrator worker mode.

**The request/response contract.** The consumer sends:

1. **StoryID** — which story to act in.
2. **Actor character and responding character(s)** — whose input this is (by default the player's character), and who should respond or react (one or more characters may respond or react in a single message). The request also names its **output viewpoint** — whose eyes the reply is written for, by default the actor's — and the acting user, recorded only for attribution.
3. **Prompt parameters** — desired length, temperature, top_p, etc.
4. **Scene context** — characters present, and recent messages for scene continuity. These help the orchestration resolve references; they are never handed to a character as-is. Each character's view of the recent scene is rebuilt from Epistrel's own history — with per-character knowledge, filtered to what that character actually perceived, so a whisper they didn't hear can't shape what they say or do, even indirectly. (Under Lite, characters share one context and see the story's recent messages verbatim — §4.6.)
5. **Player input** — what the player's character just said or did. This is the authoritative way a player's move enters history (as the turn's first beat); the recent messages above are context only and never written as history. A separate ingest call records events without generating a reply, with a client event ID so a resend is never recorded twice.

Epistrel returns the **finished, polished message**, with the claims the engine detected in it checked under the story's engine profile — never "verified" beyond those claims, and not checked structurally at all under Lite. The consumer's only other jobs are to register any external-data providers and to supply model configuration (see below). No retrieval, verification, or memory logic lives in the consumer.

**Models are external processes, configured inside Epistrel.** Generation and the internal cognition tasks run on external model servers (Ollama, vLLM, a hosted provider) reached over the OpenAI-compatible wire format. Epistrel **holds the per-purpose configuration** (endpoint, model, key, params) and **connects directly**; a runtime config API allows changes without restart. "Model-agnostic" means the endpoint is configurable, not that the consumer owns the call — the Orchestrator's re-generation loop calls a model several times per turn, so the calls must live inside Epistrel. A separate **embedding port** handles vectorization and is treated as sticky (the embedding model's dimension is baked into every stored vector).

**Storage** is PostgreSQL + pgvector (plus optional BM25), chosen so world state and memory commit in a single transaction — the property that makes "verify a claim against authoritative state" and "commit a turn atomically" possible without cross-store consistency problems.

See *02 — Technology & Architecture* for the full topology diagram and the enumerated model-purpose list.

### 4.1 Multi-character responses

A single request may name several characters who respond or react together. With per-character renders, this is not a loop-twice convenience: each character is **rendered against their own viewpoint** (one character's reply must never be contaminated by another's private thoughts), **verified against their own beliefs**, and their `latent`/commitment effects are **reconciled against one shared timeline** before the composed message is returned. This is precisely the *contradictory concurrent commitment* case — two characters whose forced facts could imply different truths about the same moment — and the Orchestrator resolves it as part of composition. (Lite writes all the characters in one combined generation, kept apart only by instruction.)

**Who goes when.** Each story chooses an interaction mode, overridable per request. In **sequential** mode — the default, and the natural fit for conversation — characters act in order and each one sees what the earlier ones visibly said and did, so Jenni can answer what Bobby just said, and physical conflicts resolve by causality: if Bobby pockets the key first, Jenni's attempt to lock it in the drawer becomes "she reaches for it, but it's already gone." Each beat is verified before the next character renders, so nobody builds on an unverified event. In **simultaneous** mode — for moments that really are concurrent, like a combat round — everyone reacts to the same instant without seeing each other, and conflicts over the same object or value are caught and settled by a per-story tie-break (request order, an initiative State, or a director call), with the losing action treated as an attempt.

**Not everyone needs a full turn.** Five characters in a room shouldn't cost five renders. Characters can be **primary** (a full render with their own speech, thoughts, and verification), **reactive** (their visible reactions — "Marcus raises an eyebrow" — narrated inside someone else's render), or **silent**. Roles default per story, can be fixed per character, and can be overridden per request. Reactive narration is observable-only and attributed to the reacting character: it never writes their private thoughts, it must respect what they actually perceived and know (Marcus can't smirk knowingly about a secret he doesn't hold), and it's non-verbal by default, with brief interjections as a per-story option. Anything more — real speech, a decision, a State change — escalates that character to a full render, unless their role is fixed or the turn's budget can't fit another full render; either way the game is told. A background character fixed as reactive is never promoted by the engine: that part of the moment waits — the game is told, and can bring them in on the next turn — rather than being narrated for them. Only the game, per request, can override a fixed role. Reactive characters still remember the scene, through the same exposure records every witness gets.

### 4.2 Extensibility and external data authorities

The Orchestrator is extensible: the consumer registers tools/Skills/MCP providers at configuration time, and the Orchestrator uses them to reach truths the game owns outside Epistrel (a player's real gold balance, the current weather, a world clock, quest flags owned by a game engine). This makes external data a **first-class fact authority** with three consequences:

- **An authority decides objective truth — not what characters know.** If the game says the balance is 300, the narrator can't say 500. But a *character* saying "I've got 500" is a claim about their own belief, not a narrator statement: they may have lost track (a legitimate false belief, if something on the record explains it), or be lying, or the model may have hallucinated. So character claims go through the same belief checks as everything else; the authority only governs objective statements, and a character learns an external value the way they learn anything — by checking, being told, or watching it happen.
- **One value per turn.** The first read of a value in a turn is pinned; generation, verification, and commit all use it. If the game's balance changes mid-turn from 300 to 250, the turn is still about 300 — verification never re-reads live. Each provider declares what happens when it's unreachable: fail the turn, use the last recorded value if it's recent enough, or treat the value as unknown for the turn. "Unknown" stops the narrator from stating the current number and stops anyone from newly learning it — it doesn't erase what characters already believe. If Bobby counted 500 gold yesterday, he can still say "I think I have 500" during an outage, and he can still lie about it; those claims are checked against his beliefs, as always.
- **The game stays the owner.** An author directive can't override a game-owned value inside Epistrel — that would just desynchronize the two systems. Providers are read-only by default: when a turn implies "spend 50 gold" against a game-owned balance, Epistrel hands that change back to the game to apply. A provider can opt into accepting writes, but Epistrel never pretends a cross-system change is atomic: it records the pending change with the turn, sends it with an idempotency key, and reports anything unconfirmed instead of assuming it happened.
- **Effects follow the timeline that made them.** A queued external change belongs to its turn. Rewind or regenerate before the dispatcher claims it, and it's cancelled — the dispatcher re-checks as it claims, before sending anything, so a payment discarded before that point can never go out. A fork never copies queued effects as live ones, so duplicating a story can't buy the sword twice. But once the game *confirms* an effect, no internal rewind can undo it: rewinding past a confirmed payment tells the game exactly which confirmed actions sat in the discarded stretch so it can decide whether to refund. And the prose can't get ahead of reality: unless the game confirms the purchase before the reply is composed, the narration describes handing over the coins, not "the purchase went through." A confirmation that arrives late — after the turn has closed — takes effect when it arrives, not back at the turn that asked for it: the engine re-checks whether the purchase still makes sense in the story as it now stands, grants the sword if it does, and otherwise flags it for the game to reconcile (a refund, say). History never silently grows a sword in a chapter that said the merchant had none — and if the player rewound past the purchase before the game answered, the confirmation doesn't reach into the new storyline at all; it waits for the author or game to apply it or refund it. If a hold on an item expires while the game is still deciding, a confirmation that comes in afterwards is never applied automatically — the sword may already have gone to someone else — so it waits for the author or game to settle it or refund it. Each pending action gets exactly one final outcome; duplicates are ignored and contradictory reports are flagged rather than acted on. When the game *does* confirm synchronously, the turn is committed in stages — the attempt, the hand-off to the game, the game's answer, the narrated outcome, the finished reply — each saved before the next begins. This is the one deliberate exception to "a turn lands all-or-nothing": ordinary turns stay atomic, while a turn waiting on the game is a short workflow of all-or-nothing stages. A crash or failure partway through resumes from the last saved stage; a purchase the game confirmed is never treated as undone just because the reply failed to compose. "Cancelled" is promised only up to one recorded moment — when the dispatcher claims the effect, before it sends anything; after that, a rewind reports the effect as possibly sent rather than pretending it can be called back.
- **Every external read is recorded; commitment references it.** The Orchestrator records *every* read as an `external_read` event. When a character commits a reference to it ("you're broke"), the committed fact (provenance `external@timestamp`) points at that `external_read` event rather than re-capturing the value — the event is the single source of truth. The live value may change later; the story's statement stays true for that moment, reconstruction replays the recorded value, and the engine never re-calls a live provider during replay.
- **External data is a trust tier and an injection surface.** It carries a high-trust provenance tier within its own domain, but tool results remain untrusted *content* — treated as data, tiered by the provider's declared authority, never as instructions.

### 4.3 Story States

Most games don't need an external data store — they need a handful of tracked values: gold, health, reputation, whether the bridge is out. Epistrel holds these itself as **States**: typed single values scoped to the story or to a character, with constraints (gold can't go negative), a visibility (the player sees their gold; an NPC's trust score stays hidden), and a schema the author defines (the engine may *suggest* "track arrows?" but never invents variables on its own). Every tracked value has an owner — the story itself, or an external provider — and both behave the same way to the rest of the engine.

Changes are declared, not guessed after the fact. A turn that says "I spent 500 gold on a new sword" carries a structured effect — gold −500, for the sword — which is checked against the constraints and committed together with the turn. Player actions are *attempts*: if the sword costs 500 and the purse holds 300, nothing changes and the shopkeeper tells you you're short. And because every State change is an event like any other, time travel simply works: rewind to message 800 and the gold comes back; fork, and each branch has its own purse. That's the decisive advantage over keeping the balance in an external system — rewinding Epistrel can't refund a game server's gold, but it can refund its own.

As with external data, a State is objective truth, not character knowledge. The player sees their visible States in the game's interface; an NPC knows someone's balance only if they have a reason to.

**Some changes wait on the game.** If buying a sword means an external payment but the sword itself lives in Epistrel, the sword is granted only once the payment is confirmed — never at the moment of the attempt, so a failed payment can't leave the player holding an unpaid sword. Selling works the other way: the sword is set aside (reserved) until the payout clears, then handed over, or returned if it fails. When one turn involves several external actions and only some succeed, each internal change follows only the actions it depends on. And when the game confirms a purchase, its answer becomes a new reading of the balance for the rest of the turn — if the game reports what's left, the narration can say "250 left"; if it only reports success, the narration says what was paid, not a remainder the engine had to guess.

**Valid bounds aren't permission.** A constraint stops gold going negative; it doesn't stop a model declaring "+100,000 gold — found treasure." So each State also says *who* may change it and *why*: which characters or passes may move it, which kinds of event justify an increase or decrease, and how large a single change may be. Finding treasure is a world event that only the director pass may introduce, within caps; anything larger needs the author. Equally, having the money isn't the same as making the purchase: buying the sword also requires the merchant to agree and the sword to be for sale, and when both purses are tracked the gold has to move from one to the other. The check runs both ways — narration that says "you pay 500" without a matching effect is caught, just as an effect with no narration is.

### 4.4 The world clock

Every story ships with a clock. Roleplay models rush: you sit down in a diner at eleven, the character mentions a meeting at five, you trade a few lines, and suddenly they "have to run to that meeting." Nothing in a transcript says that only twenty minutes have passed, so the model fills the gap with momentum. Epistrel makes story-time an explicit, tracked value — a built-in State, on by default — and everything the story records takes its time from it.

**The clock advances at a believable pace, deterministically.** Each beat advances it by how long it takes to say and do: under five words, no time; five to fifty words, one minute; more than fifty, two minutes. (The table is configurable per story.) Thoughts don't take time; simultaneous reactions count once, because they happen in the same moment. Twenty exchanges in the diner come to roughly twenty minutes — not six hours.

**Skips are explicit, estimated when needed, and bounded.** When the narration moves time forward — "They watch the rest of the movie in silence" — the render declares how long that took, or, if it doesn't, the Orchestrator estimates it from what's established (the movie started at 7:10 and runs two hours, so about ninety minutes remain) and records the estimate and its reason. The player and author can skip as far as they like, and that includes skips the player sets in motion without naming a time: if the player narrates falling asleep, the model is expected to carry them through the night and describe what they wake up to. A skip the model starts *on its own* is held to more: it must narrate something already happening in the scene, and it must stay under a cap that each scenario or story sets (24 hours by default, or zero to forbid model-initiated skips entirely). A character can't decide, out of nowhere, that "an hour later, the doorbell rings."

**Appointments are facts, and the clock enforces them.** When Jenni makes a plan — "I've got a meeting at five" when no meeting exists yet — that's a fact about the world: she's the authority on her own plans. But once the meeting exists, saying when it is is just a statement like any other: if her assistant told her five and it's really at six, she's sincerely wrong, the meeting stays at six, and only whoever owns the meeting can move it. If she then gets up to leave for it at 11:20, believing it's 11:20, that's caught and repaired; if she's using the meeting as an excuse to escape a bad date, that's a lie, and lies are allowed. Any advance that would sail past a due appointment involving someone present, or the scene itself, stops at it (a per-story setting, on by default) — a narrated skip, or just the minute or two of an ordinary exchange that happens to cross five o'clock — and when it was a skip, the narration is redone to stop there too — you're woken at six by the knock, not handed a description of the sunrise you never saw. Each appointment stops the clock once; after it surfaces it's kept, missed, cancelled, or rescheduled. A *mistaken* belief about a schedule — Marcus thinks Jenni's meeting is at four — shapes what Marcus says and does, but never stops the world.

**Retcons and uncertain clocks.** A history rewrite that adds a fact "in 2012" to a story whose clock only knows "Saturday afternoon" can't always be placed relative to an earlier checkpoint. By default such a fact is included wherever it *might* precede the checkpoint, and flagged as time-uncertain — otherwise nearly every backstory retcon in a loosely anchored story would vanish from forks. A story can instead ask the author, at rewrite time, to place each such fact before or after the relevant point.

**The clock never invents precision.** If the story only ever said "Saturday morning," the engine knows it's Saturday and somewhere between five and noon — and earlier scenes are placed by how much time has passed since — but it won't decide it's 9:47 on the 14th. Checks that depend on what was never established stay open until the story establishes it. If a meeting *might* be due because the hour was never pinned down, the clock pauses and the story is nudged to establish the time — someone checks a phone — rather than declaring the meeting has arrived. And within a single reply, time moves where the narration says it moves: "at 3:10 she starts driving; twenty minutes later she arrives" puts the departure at 3:10 and the arrival at 3:30.

**Characters know the time the way people do.** Narration about time is checked against the clock. A character's statement is checked against what *they* believe the time is: in a modern story everyone has a phone and knows the time, in a period or fantasy story they know roughly the hour, and a prisoner in a windowless cell may know nothing at all — a per-story setting. A stopped watch, being told the wrong time, or a stretch spent unconscious changes an individual character's belief. So Bobby saying "it's only three" at half past five is a sincere error if his watch stopped, a lie if he's stalling, and a mistake to repair only if he has no reason to believe it. Each character is shown their own sense of the time and their own commitments when they speak; only the orchestration sees the true clock.

Because the clock is a State, it rewinds, regenerates, and forks like everything else, and a game that runs its own clock can own it instead, with Epistrel reading it and proposing the advances.

### 4.5 Characters, scenarios, and starting a story

Stories don't start from nothing. Epistrel keeps a **catalog of characters** — reusable definitions of *who someone is*: appearance, personality and temperament, likes and dislikes, how they react to a threat, whether they lead or follow, whether they tell the truth, how they talk — plus, optionally, images and a **visual identity** — the image model, an appearance prompt, a seed, and reference pictures — that keeps new images of them looking like the same person, as far as the image model allows. A character carries no facts about any particular world; those belong to scenarios. It also keeps a catalog of **scenarios**, each a reusable starting point for a story: the world and its lore, the media that sells it, the starting clock and tracked values, what's already true, secret, or scheduled when the story opens, what drives the plot (hooks, objectives, hidden director guidance, planned reveals), and the **roles** the story needs. Those parts are kept apart by what they are: only what's true or known in the story's world becomes story fact; the plot drivers and director guidance steer the storytelling without ever counting as fact or reaching a character; settings configure the story; and the art style only shapes pictures.

Roles are slots. A family-dinner scenario might need a mother, a father, a brother, and a sister, with the player as the third sibling. A role can be **open** (pick any suitable character from the catalog, or make one up on the spot), **pre-filled** with a default you can swap, or **locked** to one character. The rule is simple: **facts come from the scenario, personality comes from the character.** The role supplies the situation — that she's your mother, that she knows the house is being sold, that she had an affair nobody knows about — and the character supplies who she is, including whether she'd lie to keep that secret. A scenario doesn't decide that an open role is deceitful; two different mothers make two different dinners. The scenario always decides which role the player plays; the player picks who fills the rest, and plays as a lightweight **persona** — a name, gender, age, and a short description they're asserting they'll play as. The story treats it like anything else characters might know: anyone who meets you sees roughly how old you are (a man in his twenties, not "23") and can draw ordinary conclusions from it, while the characters the scenario makes your family, friends, or coworkers already know your name, your age, and what you're like — so if you're known to have a short fuse, they may tread carefully. The player never plays a catalog character as their own; the platform plays those — a game can still send a line for one of them, which Epistrel checks like that character's own. Every catalog character also comes with a ready-made one-to-one scenario, for when you just want to talk to them; that companion scenario is where the character's default world lives (her history, her secrets), and it stays behind when she's cast in someone else's story. A story can start with up to 16 characters by default, a number each scenario or story can change.

Starting a story **copies** the chosen versions of the scenario and characters into it, as its very first operation, so the story stays self-contained: editing or deleting a catalog entry later never changes a story already in progress, and you can always rewind a story to its opening. A story uses as much of its scenario as its engine profile can. A Lite story gets the scenario's story content as text in its one shared prompt: the world, the roles, and what's true, with the director's notes labeled apart. Every character's secrets are in that shared prompt too, because Lite keeps characters apart only by instruction, so a scenario that depends on secrets is better served by a profile with per-character knowledge. (The public card and the art style never go into a text prompt, under any profile.) Lite can also show the clock and tracked values in that prompt when they're switched on, but only as display: the clock moves with the story's pacing and nothing is checked against it, and tracked values change only when the game or the author changes them. What Lite can't use — which character knows which secret, the facts and rules in structured form, and the clock and tracked values when they're off — is kept with the story, listed when it's created, and brought in at the start of its history if the story is later upgraded. That holds even when the upgrade doesn't reprocess the turns already played; those turns then aren't checked against it, and the upgrade says so. Nothing is silently dropped. A Lite story also can't tell when a scenario fact contradicts a character's own background: it puts the scenario's facts first, tells the model they prevail, and reports that the check didn't run. Characters who show up during play belong only to that story, unless the author chooses to promote one into the catalog. Epistrel serves every entry two ways: a small **public card** — a scenario's name, public description, and media; each role's label and short setup description; each character's name, public description, and images, all with their labels — and the full entry. A game shows players the card, so picking a scenario doesn't spoil the world, the secrets, or the character definitions behind it; the public descriptions are never fed into the story. Entries carry descriptive **labels** for search (a scenario might be *family*, *RPG*, *happy ending*; a character *bubbly*, *nervous*, *dominant*) and a **listing status** for the game's own publishing workflow (say *private*, *in review*, *public*). Epistrel stores both and filters on them when asked, but neither restricts anything: who may see or use an entry is the game's decision. None of this changes the engine itself: the catalog is a library of starting points, and a story created from it runs exactly like any other.

Images are optional — a text-only or accessibility-focused deployment needs none — and Epistrel stores whatever images and videos a deployment uses, serving large videos in pieces so players can seek. It can also make images itself: generation sits behind an adapter, starting with OpenRouter, which reaches many image models through one interface, and a game can just as well generate its own and upload them. Either way, Epistrel writes the prompt, because it knows what the generator doesn't: what a character looks like, the scenario's art style, the story's rating, and, mid-story, where everyone is and what the player's character has actually seen — so a picture of the scene is drawn only from what that character perceived, down to how old someone looks to them. The image model is never told anything else; the finished picture isn't inspected for coincidences, and pictures the game uploads itself are the game's to check. Story images sit in the story at the moment they illustrate, added on request or automatically when the story arrives somewhere new, and they rewind, regenerate, and fork with the turns they belong to. Images go through the guardrails too. The prompt is checked before anything is drawn — that check knows the true age of every character the engine tracks (under Lite, the cast), including anyone merely named in the prompt or standing in the scene, and treats anyone it can't identify and can't tell is an adult as a minor; it is the main safeguard, and what it knows stays with it, since the image model is only ever told how old someone looks. The finished picture is then held back, unstored, while a vision model checks it: a backstop whose error rates are measured and published, not assumed. Both checks are model calls, so with a hosted checker the prompt, the picture, and the true ages it is given go to that provider and are kept on its terms — outside anything Epistrel can erase. Epistrel sends the checker only what the check needs (no story text, no other secrets) and documents exactly what each model receives; a deployment that wants this data to stay in-house runs its own vision model. If no vision model is set up to check a rule that applies to images, Epistrel throws away the picture it generated; one the game uploads is stored marked unchecked. An image that arrives after its moment has passed — the turn was rewound or regenerated, or the character's description changed while it was drawing — is thrown away too. If a crash leaves it unclear whether the image service received a request, Epistrel counts the images as made, so limits err toward making fewer, and corrects the count if the real result turns up later. Every deployment has daily image limits out of the box — per turn, per story, per catalog entry, and overall — and any limit can be set to zero to switch generation off there. The limits cap how many images Epistrel asks for; they are not a spending cap. A late result or a provider returning extra images can push usage past a limit (always reported), some costs are unknown, and a request retried after an unclear crash can be billed twice.

### 4.6 Engine profiles: from Lite to Max

Not every story needs everything. A quick one-on-one story doesn't need per-character belief tracking, and some tiers of a product won't pay for it. So how hard the engine works is set per story by an **engine profile**: a saved, named set of capability modules and dials. At the bottom is **Lite**, the equivalent of how roleplay engines work today — the scenario and character descriptions as text, a custom-instructions block, pinned memories, a running memory that compresses as it grows, the last few messages, and generation settings like temperature and length — with one generation call per turn and none of the structured machinery — no claim detection or checking. Guardrails still apply if any are defined: rule-based ones cost no model call, and each guardrail set up to use a model adds a check call for each text it screens — the player's input and the reply — plus regenerations within the retry budget if one trips. The seeded minors rule uses a model, so out of the box a Lite turn makes one generation call plus those checks. Summarizing older messages happens in the background. At the top is **Max**: every capability on, at its most conservative settings, with little regard for cost. In between, every module — per-character knowledge, perception, verification and claim extraction, fact commitment, the world clock, tracked values, multi-character rendering, repair, budgets, and which model does what — can be turned up or down, and the engine checks that the combination makes sense (nested beliefs need per-character memory, for instance) before it ever runs. Who gets which profile is up to the game.

A story always follows the latest version of its profile, and changing a dial — a gate threshold, a budget, a model, the temperature — needs no backfill and takes effect from the next turn, once the game confirms the impact report (which says whether the configuration stays certified). Turning a module *on* is different: the turns already played don't have that data. Epistrel doesn't refuse the change outright; it first reports what it means — which stories are affected, what each gains or loses, what a backfill would cost — and the game confirms with `ignore_warnings`. It can then **backfill**, re-deriving the missing structure from the stored transcript while the story is paused, or go forward only and accept the gap. (These upgrade choices arrive with history rewriting; until then, a story that already has history keeps its configuration, and the impact report says so.) Either way, anything the scenario authored for the new module — a secret's holders, a scheduled event — is added at the story's start, since it was never derived from the transcript. Backfill never rewrites a single message the player saw; when the old text contradicts itself, the story's policy decides whether the latest or the earliest statement becomes canon, or asks. This is possible only because **the full transcript is always stored, under every profile** — a thousand messages into a Lite story, it can still become a Max story. Turning a capability on is also not the same as knowing it performs well: quality targets are measured for specific configurations — a profile, its models, and its thresholds — and every response says whether it ran on a configuration that has passed those evals, or one that hasn't been tested since a model or threshold changed. And the guarantees follow the profile: Lite keeps characters apart only by instruction, not structurally, and the published guarantee table says so. Guardrails sit outside all of this: if guardrail policies are defined, no profile bypasses them.

### 4.7 Concurrency and retries

Committing a turn atomically guarantees it lands all-or-nothing; it doesn't stop two requests from both generating against the same stale story and both landing as "the next message." Because generation takes seconds and several model calls, Epistrel uses optimistic concurrency rather than holding locks: each story has a **timeline revision** that advances whenever story truth changes (turns, truncation, swaps, history rewrites — but not background consolidation, so maintenance work never invalidates a turn in flight). A turn remembers the revision it started from and which records it relied on. At commit, if nothing moved, it lands; if the story moved but nothing it relied on changed, it is re-verified and lands; if something it relied on changed, it regenerates; and if the user truncated or swapped mid-generation, it is abandoned, because the old result is no longer wanted.

**How overlapping turn requests are handled is configured per story.** A roleplay story **rejects** a second request while one is generating — in a one-at-a-time conversation an overlap is almost always a mistake. A game story can **queue** them instead, because several scenes with different characters may genuinely be active at once; each queued turn generates against the state after the one before it lands.

**Changing the rules mid-turn is a conflict too.** If an author changes what a relation means, who may spend from a purse, or how much players may narrate, or an operator makes a game provider read-only, while a turn is generating, the turn is re-checked under the new rules before it commits — and regenerated if it no longer passes. Settings that only shape future work (which model to use, retry budgets, the turn-order mode) never disturb a turn already in progress; it finishes under the settings it started with.

A turn's record of what it relied on includes what it *didn't* find: if it checked for a lock, found none, and narrated opening the door, an amendment adding a lock mid-generation counts as a change it depended on, and the turn regenerates. Likewise, a rewrite plan is re-checked for dependents that appeared after it was previewed.

Every mutating request accepts an **idempotency key**, which matters more here than in most systems: generation is nondeterministic, so a network retry without one would produce a *different* message and could append it twice. With a key, a retry within the result window (24 hours by default) returns the original result, and later a reference to it — never a re-run. Rewrite previews carry the revision they were computed against and are re-checked at final commit, so an approved plan can't silently apply to a story that changed while the author was answering questions. And background jobs check that every input they read is still present and unchanged before committing — if history was rewritten under them, they discard their result and recompute.

### 4.8 Content ratings, ages, and guardrails

Every character and scenario carries a **content rating**, and so does every story. The ratings are the deployment's own — Epistrel seeds Everyone, Teen, Mature, and Explicit, and a deployment can rename, add, or reshape them — and each is more than a label. It carries **prompt guidance** that steers every generation toward the right register, and **guardrails** that enforce it. Ratings can inherit guardrails from one another, so Everyone can take everything Teen has and add rules for younger players, and they don't have to form a line. A story runs at its scenario's rating, and every character in it plays at that rating whatever its own: an Explicit character cast in an Everyone scenario plays at Everyone, with a warning when the story is set up. Mismatches warn; they don't block.

A rating isn't access control — who may play an Explicit story is the game's decision — and a story can't drift out of its rating on its own, because the guardrails keep a Teen story from turning Explicit. Only the game changes a story's rating, by asking. Loosening it (Teen to Explicit) happens at once, no questions asked. Tightening it (Explicit to Teen) is checked first: the whole history is run past the stricter guardrails, and if anything in it fails, the change is refused. Turns that were rewound or set aside are checked when they would come back: undoing a rewind, swapping back to another continuation, or splitting one off into its own story first re-checks anything not yet checked against the story's current rules — after a tightening, a new policy, or an import — and is refused if it fails. To help authors choose, Epistrel can review a scenario or character against every rating in the deployment and return a table: for each rating, whether the content fits and, if not, exactly which of that rating's policies it breaks — and nothing else. It never calls a story "too tame" for Explicit; a rating fits unless the content breaks its rules.

Every character has a **true age**, known to the engine from the moment the character exists — except in Lite, which tracks no entities: a character improvised in a Lite story's narration has no age the engine holds, and age-conditioned guardrails there rely on the cast's true ages and the model's reading of the text. Catalog characters and personas declare theirs; a character who turns up mid-story gets one on the spot, chosen plausibly — the old man who stops to help with a flat tire is 64; a student on a college campus is somewhere from 18 to 23, but 18 if it's freshman orientation. The age isn't announced: others see roughly how old someone looks. It matters because some guardrails apply at every rating: Epistrel ships with a global rule that no sexual content involving a minor, or in a minor's presence, is ever produced — Explicit included. It is a seeded rule rather than hard-coded, so a deployment's administrator can change it; it ships on, and turning it off is the deployment's decision and responsibility. Global guardrails also apply to what comes **in**. In-character steering handles most things gracefully — a teen character texting "can't talk about that, my mom reads my texts lol" — but text from the player that breaks a global rule never enters the story: the request comes back with an error naming the rule, so it can be rewritten.

## 5. The memory model

Memory is tiered, matching how recall actually works:

- **Working memory** — the recent scene **as this character perceived it**, rebuilt from the story's history for each character before they speak: what they heard appears in full, what they only saw happen (a whisper across the room) appears as that, what they weren't there for doesn't appear at all, and nobody else's private thoughts ever do. It's always in that character's context and derived on the fly, never separately stored. (Under the Lite engine profile, where characters share one context, the recent messages are included verbatim instead — §4.6.)
- **Episodic memory** — discrete, timestamped events ("Jenni hung her coat in the closet"), embedded for retrieval, scored by relevance, recency, and importance.
- **Semantic memory** — distilled standing facts derived from episodes ("Marcus distrusts the player"). A dynamic, growing lorebook.
- **Reflections** — higher-level abstractions from a background consolidation pass ("the park crew were fun but unreliable"). Essential: abstract questions only match abstract memories, so Epistrel manufactures them.

Every memory carries **provenance** (author/GM canon, system-consolidated, in-world assertion, or external authority), a **trust/authority tier**, **visibility** (the epistemic scope), **importance**, and three coordinates — *narrative position* (the message that introduced it, plus whether it is still an active member of the timeline), *valid-time* (when it is true in-story), and *transaction-time* (when it was recorded/changed) — enabling clean rewind, retcon, and audit (§12).

Consolidation runs off the hot path, compressing episodic detail into semantic facts and reflections over time, so the world is never stuffed whole into any context — the memory-collapse problem (§1.1) dissolves because retrieval is always selective and abstraction is always available. Consolidation never widens who can see something: a reflection is visible only to characters who could see *every* memory it was built from, so two private memories can't be merged into a reflection that reveals both. It never changes what *kind* of knowledge something is, either: if Bobby said something in public, the reflection remembers that Bobby said it — not that it's true. And a stored reflection is reused as-is when a story is reconstructed or forked; running the model again is a new, separately recorded operation, done only when its sources change.

**What's searchable is bounded; what's true isn't thrown away.** A long story keeps growing its history — that's unavoidable and honest. What stays bounded is what retrieval searches by default: each character has a budget of "hot" memories, and once a stretch of episodes has been summarized, the oldest, least important details retire from that hot set. Retired doesn't mean deleted or forgotten by the world: the facts still count for verification, the history still shows them, and asking about "that game a month ago" can pull the old episodes back up by date or name. Commitments, open mysteries, and appointments aren't searchable memories at all — they're structured records that always reach the model through their own channels — so only author-pinned memories are exempt, and pins are capped both per character and per story, so the bound holds however many characters a story has. Retirement happens in the background, so a burst can briefly run over budget; each such overflow has a fixed deadline from the moment it starts — more records arriving don't push it back — and the overflow is reported until it's cleared.

## 6. The epistemic model (the differentiator)

Generic agent-memory systems model "the agent" and "the user." Epistrel models **N characters, each with a partial and fallible view of one shared world.** For each character it tracks **self-world beliefs** (what they believe true), **social-world beliefs** (beliefs about other characters, including nested beliefs — "Bobby believes Jenni is hiding something", or "Jenni thinks Bobby thinks it was Marcus at the door"), **false beliefs** (held as first-class truth for that character), and **could-have-known constraints** (time/presence/informed limits on what they may reference).

Objective canon is distinct from every character's beliefs. A fact can be true in the world, known to one character, believed falsely by another, and unknown to a third — simultaneously, and all queryable. This model is continuously populated by the turn's write path (§7–8) and enforced at retrieval: a character's render context contains only their own viewpoint. The consequences are the expressive powers the category has lacked — secrets, lies, dramatic irony, mysteries, and intrigue that hold up across a long game (§1.3).

**How beliefs form — hearing is not believing.** Storing that everyone heard Jenni say "Olivia sent the text" does not establish what anyone *believes*. A public event records only *exposure* (and the speech act — that Jenni asserted it); what each witness does with it — believe her, doubt her, already know otherwise, or merely register that she *claimed* it — is a separate, per-character transition. Belief acquisition is therefore modeled as explicit typed operations, each carrying dated provenance: **observation** (I saw it), **testimony** (someone told me — gated by trust, and never auto-accepted), **inference** (I concluded it from what I knew), **discovery** (I found it out), **correction** (I was wrong, now I know better), and **forgetting**. This is what lets one proposition be simultaneously false in canon, asserted by Jenni, believed by Bobby, disbelieved by Marcus, and unknown to Sarah — five typed records, not one. Confidence is dual: a categorical level (unknown / suspected / believed / known) always, and an optional numeric scale for games that need "70% sure," with the category a projection of the number when present.

Two safeguards make this honest. Belief formation is **lazy and salience-proportionate** — a background witness gets only an exposure record, and their belief is resolved only if something later depends on it — which bounds the per-event fan-out. And lazy resolution is **pinned to the exposure moment, not the present**: when Bobby's belief about Jenni's claim is finally resolved, it is decided against what Bobby knew *at the time he heard her*, reconstructed as-of that point — down to the beat within the message — so a truth revealed to canon forty messages later, or even two beats later in the same message, cannot leak backward into the earlier decision. Deferral saves computation, not information. If Bobby *himself* later learns the truth, that is a new, separately-dated correction — and the history then correctly shows he believed Jenni until he found out. A resolved belief keeps two anchors: *when Bobby heard it* (which fixes what evidence counts and from when he believes it) and *where the engine decided* (which fixes which timeline the decision belongs to). Viewed from turn 900, Bobby has believed Jenni since turn 10; rewind to before the turn where that was decided, or swap to a continuation that never decided it, and the question is open again. (Once resolved, a belief is logged and established, not recomputed; it is the same bitemporal machinery that powers rewind and recorded external reads, doing double duty again.)

**Who gets modeled — and how much weight they carry.** A character is modeled (given a viewpoint and memory) when it is *directly interacted with* — talked to, waved at, exchanged with — or *explicitly made salient*; mere appearance (a jogger passing in the park) models nothing. Crucially, *existence and narrative weight are different axes*: modeling is liberal and cheap, narrative weight is earned. The delivery driver Jenni interacts with becomes a full modeled character — with his own private thoughts and memory — but stays low-weight unless the story develops him. This is how a recurring minor character (the same driver every few days) quietly accrues a real relationship over fifty deliveries, exactly the long-horizon texture open roleplay can't sustain — while the engine consciously restrains the model from inflating every minor character into a plot pivot.

**Minor characters are full characters, just sparse.** The driver's private observations — that Jenni looked sad, that he liked seeing her in leggings — are written to *his* partition and shown to no one: not to Jenni, and not to the player — not quoted, not paraphrased, not narrated as "he thought she looked sad." What the player *can* see is whatever the driver visibly does: a sympathetic glance is his own action, and reading something into it is fair inference, not a leak. The rendered message exposes only what he did (smiled, handed over the package, turned to leave). Those private memories simply persist, and may legitimately **resurface later** from his own viewpoint — six months on, in a scene with Jenni and the player, he recalls that she seemed sad the first time they met, and it is *correct* because it tracks what was actually recorded. That grounded resurfacing is encouraged; it is the payoff of modeling interiority. It is distinct from confabulation — inventing a "memory" with no backing record — which verification catches. The discriminator is provenance: a recorded partition entry behind the recall, or none.

**The player is the one exception.** The player exists as a modeled character — every other character holds memories of and beliefs about them — but the engine stores no player *interiority*, because the player's memory is their own, in their head. Their words and actions are recorded as others witness them; there is no player `think`, no player partition, and the asymmetry machinery simply doesn't run for the one participant who is human.

## 7. The structured turn contract

Epistrel does not treat a free-text blob as a turn. The render step returns structure, and each channel routes to a different destination because each has a different *audience*:

- **`say`** — spoken words. The observable act goes to everyone authoritatively co-located; the words go to whoever actually hears them (everyone in the location by default; with full perception routing, fewer for a whisper and more for a validated eavesdrop or a phone call) → episodic memory for those perceivers.
- **`think`** — inner thoughts. Private → the thinker's partition only. **Never retrievable into another character's context.**
- **`do`** — observable actions. Seen by everyone authoritatively co-located → episodic memory for those perceivers.
- **`latent`** — determinate-but-hidden truths introduced this turn (who really sent the text, why the door is knocking). → objective canon, visibility-scoped to whoever knows.
- **state effects** — declared changes to tracked values ("gold −500, for the sword"). → validated against the State's constraints and committed with the turn, or handed back to the game for values it owns (§4.3); narrated time skips are declared the same way, as advances of the world clock (§4.4).

**Presence is not perception, and an utterance has two parts.** Being in the room is a *candidate* to perceive, not a guarantee — whispers, closed doors, foreign languages, and remote channels all break the naive "everyone present hears everything." So routing is by perception, and every utterance splits into an **observable envelope** (that speech occurred, who spoke, to whom, the visible manner and reaction, the surrounding context) and a **content payload** (the words). The envelope routes to everyone authoritatively co-located, always; the content routes by audience — the room by default, narrowed by an explicit whisper to its target, widened by an explicit eavesdrop or a phone call — which the engine checks are physically possible (a shared wall or door, a call actually in progress) before granting anyone the words. This split needs full perception routing (the Full and Max presets); with co-location-only perception, as in Standard, everyone in the room hears everything, and without perception — Lite, or a structured story with shared character knowledge — a character's view shows every beat except characters' private thoughts. With full routing, when Jenni whispers to Marcus in front of Bobby, Bobby witnesses the *act* at full fidelity — she crossed the room, touched his arm, Marcus stiffened — and gets *none* of the words. That content-less observation is a real memory with real consequences: Bobby might ask what they're whispering about, file it away, or feel a flash of jealousy — all grounded in what he actually saw, none of it leaking the words he never heard. The rule stays deliberately dumb — co-location by default, deviations only when the render explicitly makes them, never a perception model guessing at distraction or acuity — and it is driven by *authoritative* positions, not the consumer's scene roster, so a mistaken or manipulated roster can never grant a character knowledge it shouldn't have. The same routing decides **what the player is shown**: every response is produced for an explicit viewpoint — normally the player's own character — so if the player is Bobby, the returned message says Jenni whispered something to Marcus and does not contain the words, in the narrative or in the beat list. No separate belief store is needed for the player; their character's position is enough. An all-seeing view exists too, returned only when explicitly asked for; which users may ask for it is the game's decision (§16).

Two rules keep this honest. **Utterances are stored as speech acts, not facts:** when Jenni says "it was Olivia," Epistrel stores *that she asserted it* — not "the text was from Olivia." The claim is quarantined as dialogue content and never promoted to world-truth, which is what keeps characters' lies from poisoning canon (§1.4). And **`think` is a *checked* intent signal, not a self-authenticating one:** it declares the character's reasoning so the validator can tell an intentional lie from a false belief from a hallucination (§10) — but it is validated against the character's engine-held belief state, never trusted on its own word, because otherwise a model could manufacture both an error and the thought that excuses it (§10).

## 8. The commitment lifecycle

A fact moves through **introduced → pending → committed → (possibly) revised.** The hard questions are *what* forces commitment and *when*.

- **Salience, not event type, triggers commitment.** A one-off car horn is texture and needs no truth. Car doors opening and closing all night is a *signal* — placed to be noticed — and a signal must have a referent. The moment narration makes something salient, Epistrel commits (a) an observation in the noticing character's partition and (b) the underlying truth in canon, scoped away from them until revealed — seeding a character-aware mystery (§1.5).
- **Commit lazily — the instant a consequence depends on the value.** Jenni's buzzing phone commits immediately because she laughs, hides it, and has thoughts about it. A coin flipped and caught but not yet reacted to stays an uncommitted **thunk**. "Someone knocked" commits for everyone who heard it; "who and why" is either **determined but unknown to the hearers** (if the knocker is a modeled character acting on a decided intent — Bobby knows it's him, Jenni doesn't) or a **genuinely undetermined global thunk** (if no one has decided who's there yet).
- **Unknown is not undetermined — and determined facts have one value.** This distinction is load-bearing. *Determined vs undetermined* is a property of world state (global: has the truth been fixed?). *Known vs unknown* is a property of a viewpoint (per-character: is it in their partition?). Once the knocker is Bobby, the identity is determined, globally; Jenni is merely *unknown* — she carries *absence*, not an alternative value. The engine must never let Jenni's next message resolve a *different* knocker, because "who knocked at that moment" is a single-valued question with one determined answer, and characters vary only in whether they hold it. (That's a rule about one answer per proposition at a time, not a ban on many-valued relationships: Bobby can know many people or have visited many places; each of those facts simply has its own single truth value.) (If Jenni *believes* it was Marcus, that's a separate belief record she owns, diverging from canon — never a second value of the knock.)
- **Hidden is not the same as undetermined.** A hidden fact (the text's true sender) is *decided but visibility-scoped*: sticky to any *later* message's regeneration, which must not re-roll it. An undetermined fact (the coin in the air) is *not yet decided*: fluid, and regeneration may resolve it differently. The knob separating them is the commit trigger — did any character's `think`/`do` depend on it yet? (Stickiness is relative to the message being regenerated: a committed fact re-rolls freely when you regenerate the message that committed it, and only holds fixed against messages after it — see the next point and FR-COMMIT-8.)
- **The commit boundary is the regeneration boundary.** Because facts are events, regenerating a message reverts that message's committed facts and replays; earlier-committed facts are before the checkpoint and stay fixed; thunks re-resolve freely. **Regeneration varies the undetermined and preserves the determined** — the behavior users want and that no current platform provides (§1.5).
- **Resolving a genuine thunk: any character, once, optionally — and small.** Any character's `think`/`do` that first needs an undetermined value resolves it, once, globally; Jenni opening the door resolves the knocker. Resolution is *optional* — if nothing ever needed it (Jenni doesn't answer), it may stay unresolved forever. And a *late* resolution inherits the weight its deferral implies: if the knocker had mattered, it would have been committed up front, so a deferred knock is steered toward the insignificant (a package delivery, not a long-lost brother) and away from an existing character who "should have known". That is steering, not a rule: such a binding is flagged — the longer the story runs before resolution, the more strongly — because backdating knowledge into a character who acted a year without it is an epistemic hole, not a twist. This restrains the model's tendency to inflate every loose thread into a revelation. But "undetermined" doesn't mean "free": naming the knocker creates facts about the past — Bobby was at that door at that moment, and has known ever since that he knocked. So every resolution, however it's made, is checked against what's already recorded: if Bobby was across town, or later sincerely asked Jenni who was at her door, the binding conflicts. A model-chosen resolution that conflicts is simply not allowed. An **author directive** is the deliberate exception to the insignificance bias — "actually, it's George, her ex" on a regeneration — and when George has no conflicting history it costs nothing to rewrite. If the author names someone whose history does conflict, the author's choice still stands, but it goes through the normal rewrite process, with those conflicts surfaced, rather than slipping in unchecked.

A **promissory ledger** tracks *salient* references awaiting commitment, so a turn cannot close while a signal dangles; unforced background thunks carry no such obligation.

## 9. Retrieval and resolution

Retrieval resolves *before* it searches: (1) **query understanding** resolves relative time to a story-time range, event type, and intent, using recent dialogue for coreference; (2) **locate the occasion** runs a structured time-and-type query over the episode layer — no vectors, because time and type are metadata; (3) **resolve participants** turns "those guys" into entity IDs from the located episode; (4) **retrieve assessments** reads standing character profiles and runs vector search scoped to those entities and that window.

Retrieval is **hybrid** (vector + lexical/BM25 for exact proper-noun matches) and **viewpoint-scoped** (a character sees only their partition plus objective-known facts). Scoring blends semantic similarity, recency decay, and importance in a single query. This solves the abstract/referential/temporal recall failure (§1.2), and it reduces pressure on retrieval precision because verification (§10) is a cheaper backstop for anything retrieval misses.

## 10. Validation and repair

Epistrel is both a *retriever* (forward: context → memory) and a **verification oracle** (reverse: claim → consistent? contradicted? novel?). The Orchestrator runs generate → detect claims → verify → repair. Verification can only check claims that were detected, so detection runs in layers on every reply — the declared structure, cheap deterministic detectors (possession, amounts, times, names, and anything matching a secret the speaker shouldn't know), and by default a model pass over the prose, including the final composed message; how many claims slip past detection is measured separately from how accurately detected claims are judged; the Engine Core supplies the grounding.

**What is guaranteed, and what is measured.** It's worth being precise, because natural language can't be verified the way a database row can. Some things are **structural guarantees** that don't depend on any model: a character's prompt is assembled only from what that character is authorized to know, so a secret that was never put in front of them can't shape what they say; every state change, permission, ownership rule, and timeline operation is checked deterministically; every claim that *is* detected is checked against the right holder's view; commits are atomic and replayable. Other steps are **model-dependent and measured**, not guaranteed: finding every claim in free prose (detection recall has a target, not a promise), noticing that something is salient, estimating how long a narrated activity took, judging fuzzy entailment, and recognizing a paraphrased or indirect disclosure that no exact-match check would catch, recognizing sexual content for an age-conditioned guardrail, suggesting a content rating, writing image prompts, and checking generated images. Output scanning for leaks is therefore a second line of defense on top of the structural one, not proof that leaks can't happen — and a test run with zero observed leaks is reported as evidence with an upper bound, never as a zero rate. These guarantees also depend on the story's engine profile (§4.6): the structural ones need the modules that provide them. So the honest summary is: the model proposes; the engine validates every structured claim, effect, and permission it can see, keeps characters' contexts structurally separate, and measures what it can't prove. Privacy is also a promise about the request, not the requester: Epistrel guarantees what a named viewpoint receives, and the game decides who may name which viewpoint (§16). Creative quality sits outside the guarantee too: Epistrel makes sure each character's personality and the scenario's hooks and planned reveals reach the right prompts, but whether a character stays in voice or a story follows its hooks is up to the model, and isn't held to a floor. Epistrel does report how each configuration does on voice, story progress, repetition, and length, so games can compare models and profiles. And it holds its own hand in the prose to account: across its evaluation sets, text it has repaired, stitched together from several characters, or polished must, on balance, read no worse than the model's first draft — an aggregate target that is measured, not a promise about every single message. Players can rate any message from 1 to 5, with reasons such as *out of character*, *repetitive*, or *forgot something*. Those ratings never touch the story; they show the people running the game where things go wrong, flag likely memory and guardrail misses for review, and — only if the deployment opts in — become test cases. The same honesty applies to cost: a turn with several characters can need several model calls each — writing, claim extraction, a repair, a fuzzy check — so every turn runs under a call budget and a deadline. When either runs out, the engine sheds the optional work first (polish, extra repair attempts, time estimates), falls back to its safe responses rather than skipping its checks, and tells the game which shortcuts it took. The checks are set aside before anything is written — a claim check for every beat, every guardrail check that uses a model (once on the player's input and once on each piece of output it screens), and, in a story wired to an external system that confirms actions, the narration and checks of the outcome — so the turn is planned to fit them: extra characters become reactions or wait for the next turn, and only a budget too small for even one character's render and checks is refused up front — never a check quietly skipped. Full-turn latency depends mostly on the model you run, so it is measured and published per model rather than promised.

- **What "contradicts" means is defined, not guessed.** Every claim is reduced to a *proposition* — who, which relation, what, true or false, over what span of story-time (or at what instant, for a one-off event) — about *entities* with stable identities, where even "the stranger is Bobby" is a fact with its own holder. Each relation is registered with its semantics: whether it has one answer *per something specific* (the institutions that awarded a particular degree; where you live right now) or many (schools you've enrolled at, teams you've played on, degrees you hold), whether it changes over time, what excludes it (alive vs dead), and what it implies. Distinct facts stay distinct: playing on a college's team, being enrolled there, and being awarded its degree are three relations, and nothing links them unless the author declares a rule that does. A contradiction is then a rule, not a judgment: the same claim negated, two different answers to a one-answer question about the *same* thing over overlapping time, adding to a set the author has declared complete, or a declared exclusion. Support is stricter than overlap: canon that Bobby lived in Ohio from 2010 to 2014 doesn't support "Bobby lived in Ohio from 2000 to 2020" — it supports the part it covers, and the rest is treated as a new claim. Different answers at *different* times aren't a conflict — people move. Missing information isn't a contradiction either; it's simply new. The engine ships a core vocabulary of relations; authors can declare more; and a relation the engine has never seen is registered with the weakest possible meaning (many answers, no exclusions, no implications), so it can never manufacture an *inferred* contradiction — though "he apprenticed with this smith" and "he never apprenticed with this smith" still conflict, because that's plain negation. Anything subtler goes to the model.
- **Three-way verdict.** `verify(claim, viewpoint)` returns *supported*, *contradicted*, or *novel*. Contradicted → repair. Supported → accept. Novel → the model filled a gap, which in open fiction is often desirable invention; Epistrel decides whether it should have been anchored to an existing fact and, if acceptable, **canonizes** it (write-back) so it is consistent forever after. The validator is a canon-growth mechanism, not only a filter. The deterministic check resolves most claims outright; it escalates to the entailment model only on the fuzzy residual, through a gate that launches conservatively (because a wrong `supported` silently corrupts canon while a needless escalation only costs a call) and is **instrumented so it can be tightened down over time** on the claim-shapes that prove safe — paying for continuity now and scaling cost down on evidence.
- **Viewpoint-awareness resolves the trichotomy.** Every utterance is labeled on three independent axes: is it **objectively** true, is it **consistent with what the speaker believes**, and was it meant **sincerely**, deceptively, or as a joke. That separates cases a single "truthful / lie" label can't: a **sincere error** (she honestly believes something false → canonical for her), a **lie** (she says something she doesn't believe, to mislead → keep it; record a **divergence** between what she said and what she believes), an **attempted lie that happens to be true**, and a lie about something the speaker is herself wrong about. A **hallucination** is a claim with no legitimate basis at all → repair. Objective claims about tracked values verify against their owner — a story State or an external authority (§4.2–4.3) — while a character's own claims about them still verify against that character's beliefs.
- **`think` cannot manufacture its own justification.** The trichotomy only works if the discriminator is independent — and a single model generating both the false claim *and* the thought that excuses it is not. So a declared false belief is **not** accepted on its own assertion: an established false belief (already in her partition, with provenance) is fine, but a *new* one is legitimate only if it traces to a **permitted cause whose antecedent was independently verified before the belief depends on it** — someone misinformed her earlier, she misperceived something recorded, she's a modeled unreliable narrator, or the author declared it. No verified antecedent → it's a hallucination wearing a justification → repair. A declared *lie* likewise needs a legitimately formed belief underneath to deviate from — though that belief needn't be correct; characters can lie about things they're wrong about. The line is **self-attestation, not timing**: cause and effect can sit in the same turn, as long as the cause was verified first. If Bobby says "The bridge collapsed" and that beat passes verification, Jenni can believe him and answer "Then we'll take the ferry" a beat later, in the same message. Within one character's render, a change of mind is declared as ordered steps — she *sees* the bridge is down, *believes* it collapsed, *heads* for the ferry — each verified before the next may lean on it. What's never allowed is a belief whose only support is itself. `think` reasons over belief state the engine supplies; it does not redefine it.
- **Least-invasive repair ladder:** trim/de-stutter → shallow substitution → regenerate-under-constraints → in-character transform → safe fallback. A self-correcting stutter is already factually correct, so the fix is cosmetic. A contradiction with no cascade is a swap. A contradiction whose justification cascades is **not** a string swap: the Engine Core returns the contradicted fact's **neighborhood** (`Bobby → OSU → Ohio → cold winters`) and the Orchestrator regenerates the affected span under those facts as hard constraints, so "went to Florida for college and played on their team — loved the year-round outdoor tennis" becomes a coherent "played club tennis indoors through the Ohio winters." (Note what makes that a contradiction. It is not "one school per person": people enroll at several schools, hold several degrees, and playing on a team says nothing by itself about where anyone was enrolled or graduated. It's that the author recorded OSU as Bobby's *only* college for those years. Without that statement — or a story rule that varsity players are enrolled where they play — "played on Florida's team" is simply new backstory, permission-checked like any other, and a summer camp at Florida contradicts nothing.)
- **Guardrails live in the immutable policy store**, evaluated on every message regardless of memory, because the threat model is that memory gets poisoned. Output enforcement is an **in-character transform** (a natural deflection in the character's voice), not a refusal; player input that breaks an input rule is rejected with an error naming the rule (§4.8). The poisoning loop — plant content, ask a character to repeat it, retrieval keeps surfacing it, the guardrail keeps failing — is broken by **quarantine/sanitize** (which changes the input so the loop converges) plus a **hard retry budget** that falls back to a canned in-character deflection. The invariant: **policy never yields to memory.** Policies are layered — global, then the story's content rating, then the story's own — and lower layers can add rules but never relax them; global rules also screen what the player sends in (§4.8).

- **Unknown is not permission.** A claim with no supporting or contradicting record isn't automatically fine to canonize. Who is asserting it matters: Bobby's render can add a detail of his own past or describe the room, but it can't invent Jenni's secret, decide what the player does, move a State outside a validated effect, or set a value the game owns. Unpermitted novelty is repaired, not canonized.
- **The polish step is verified too.** The final pass that stitches beats into a readable message is itself a model call, so it's constrained to ordering, joining, and connective prose, then checked: every claim must trace to a verified beat, every line must keep its speaker, and nothing from anyone's private thoughts may appear. If it fails, the response falls back to the verified beats assembled mechanically.

This contains the model's unreliability (§1.4): under a profile that verifies, every claim the engine detects is checked against authoritative truth and rules before it reaches the player. How many claims slip past detection is measured, not promised, and Lite does no structured checking at all.

## 11. Author/user directives and reconciliation

Users steer: out-of-character instructions, scene direction, regeneration with guidance, and explicit fact edits. Epistrel handles these through an **authority hierarchy**: author/GM directives outrank narrative canon, which outranks in-world character assertions.

- **Classify the intervention.** An ephemeral steer ("make this tense") is transient generation guidance and is **not** persisted as canon. A factual directive ("Bobby went to UCLA") is durable and wins over existing canon — except values the game owns, which only the game can change (§4.2).
- **A retcon is a cascade, not an overwrite.** Changing `OSU → UCLA` orphans dependent facts. Epistrel runs the retroactive repair ladder: walk the dependency neighborhood, auto-fix incidental mentions, flag cascaded justifications for regeneration, and **surface irreducible conflicts to the author** ("UCLA conflicts with three facts — rewrite, drop, or keep?") rather than silently corrupting. Conflict detection is the same `verify` oracle pointed at the directive.
- **Bitemporal retcon.** The change reads as *always having been true* in story-time while recording *when and by whom* it changed — a clean in-world history plus a full, reversible audit trail.
- **Not every dependency is the same.** The cascade follows how something depended on the old fact. A decision *caused* by it is re-evaluated; narration that *presupposed* it is fixed; a passing *mention* is touched up. But a line someone *said* about it stays exactly as said. If Bobby, believing he went to OSU, once lied that he went to UCLA, retconning his school to UCLA doesn't rewrite the line or his intent: it becomes an attempted lie that happened to be true — objectively true, still contrary to what he believed, still meant to deceive — unless the rewrite also changes what Bobby believes. The author is told, since the scene may have lost its point. Likewise, Marcus, who believed Bobby's claim, still believes it; Sarah, who saw the OSU diploma, has her belief updated.

Because the structured store cannot be hand-edited, the author-facing form of this is a **natural-language history side-channel** — a side-chat with the story, separate from the roleplay, where the author says what to change ("Bobby went to UCLA, not Ohio State") and the engine works out the rewrite. It parses the intent, computes the blast radius with `verify` + `fact_neighborhood`, and resolves conflicts *conversationally* ("they met at an Ohio State game — should that become UCLA, elsewhere, or drop it?"). Two behaviors are consumer-configurable and orthogonal, defaulting to the safe end: `resolution_mode` (`synchronous` pauses and asks per conflict before committing; `best_effort` auto-resolves, commits, and returns a report; `threshold` asks only on high-severity conflicts) and `preview` (`preview_first` returns the plan for approval; `apply_directly`). Underneath every mode, a rewrite is one **atomic, reversible** transaction within a single story — never half-applied, always undoable. This reflects a standing principle: wherever safety trades against smoothness, Epistrel exposes the policy and the consumer chooses.

This solves silent retroactive corruption (§1.6) and turns reconciliation into authorial collaboration.

## 12. Time travel: rewind, fork, and regenerate

**Three coordinates, each with one job.** Time travel only works if three different "times" are kept apart. *Narrative position* is where on the message timeline a record entered — the turn that introduced it, not its raw event sequence number, because background work and audit records interleave with turns in recording order. *Valid-time* is when something is true in the world. *Transaction-time* is when the engine recorded, changed, or discarded it. They look alike while you play forward, and they split apart the moment something is recorded *later* about an *earlier* point — which is exactly what a retcon or a flashback is. Checkpoints, truncation, regeneration, and forks run on narrative position; retcons and flashbacks live in valid-time; audit and undo use transaction-time. None substitutes for another.

Within a story, rewind and regenerate are one primitive viewed two ways: **the current corrected history through checkpoint N** — every record introduced at or before message N, still an active member of the timeline, with every history correction in force. Deleting message 801 (and everything after) truncates to N; regenerating rewinds to the last checkpoint and replays. Every event belongs to an *operation* — a turn, a consolidation job, a rewrite session, or a control action like truncate or fork — and carries a category saying what kind of record it is. "Message 801" is simply the records its turn operation introduced; nothing needs a separate message-to-memory link, and a reflection computed between turns, a rejected draft, or a rewrite discussion never gets confused for part of a message. Rejected drafts and rewrite discussions are audit records that story views never read. A reflection computed after message 800 from earlier sources counts as part of the corrected history through 800 (it's a cache over that history), but not as part of what existed *at* 800 in the audit view — and consolidation jobs declare exactly which records they read, and throw away their output if any of those records were deleted while the job ran. Some changes land *between* replies rather than in one — the game resetting a value, a late payment confirmation — so a checkpoint can be either "right after reply N" (the default for rewinding and forking) or "after reply N and the changes that came in after it." Whatever happens in that gap is stamped with the story's clock at that moment, so a confirmation that arrives after the game moved the clock forward is dated at the new time.

In-story truncation **discards; it does not delete — and it uses timeline membership, not valid-time.** Rewinding to N marks every record introduced after N as discarded, rather than erasing it, which keeps the rewind reversible and the audit trail intact. It can't be done by closing valid-time: if message 900 says "Bobby broke his arm when he was seven," that fact's valid-time begins decades before checkpoint 800, so trimming valid-time after 800 would never remove it. Membership is its own field for exactly that reason. Derived memory follows a cascade: a reflection is included only if all its source records are, and because derived memory is a projection, excluded reflections aren't surgically edited — they're rebuilt by re-running consolidation at the new head. A reflection distilled from messages 300–900 does not survive a truncation to 800 intact, but re-consolidation rebuilds the right one from the surviving 300–800 episodes. External-authority reads are recorded as events so this replay is deterministic — reconstruction replays the recorded value, never re-calling a live provider (which would return today's balance, not the as-of-N value).

**Forking and deletion treat a story as a fully self-contained dataset.** A fork is a *full, independent copy* of the source story's data as of N under a new `story_id` — a bounded operation, duplicating one story's rows in a single transaction (the same order of work as the message-copying today's roleplay engines do). There are no cross-story references, which is what makes the two operations players rely on both trivial and correct: **deleting a story is just deleting its data** (no reference-counting, no effect on anything else — exactly what you want when users create and discard stories constantly), and a fork can **rewrite its own history independently** without touching the original, which is impossible if forks share ancestry. The two are the decisive reasons full-copy is the right model, not shared storage. The same property makes a story **portable**: it exports as a single bundle and imports into any Epistrel deployment as a new story — never overwriting one — after that deployment's erasures, safety policies, and chosen content rating have been applied to it. Any external action still pending is handed to the game for reconciliation, never sent a second time.

**History rewrites are amendments, not narrative events.** The ordering case shows why this matters: play to 1,000, rewrite Bobby's childhood through the side-channel (recorded at 1,001), *then* fork at 800. A naive "fold everything through event 800" would drop the rewrite — it was recorded after 800 — and hand the fork the original childhood. Instead, a side-channel rewrite is recorded as an *amendment*: a correction with a transaction-time but no place on the message timeline. A checkpoint view includes an amendment's effect whenever it corrects a record introduced at or before the checkpoint, or introduces a fact (say, "Bobby had a sister all along") whose valid-time falls before the checkpoint's story-time. So the fork at 800 gets the corrected childhood, while the parts of the rewrite that touched messages 801–1,000 simply don't apply to it. If an amendment kept in a fork turns out to have depended on something after the checkpoint, the same conflict check the rewrite channel already uses surfaces it. Forking still cuts both ways: a rewrite applied to one side after forking never reaches the other. Forks are snapshots in time.

**Undoing a rewrite is itself a checked rewrite.** If one rewrite made Bobby's school UCLA and a later one added his UCLA graduation, undoing the first can't just flip it back and leave the graduation dangling. Undo computes its own blast radius against the story as it is now, finds the graduation as a dependent, and asks (or, in best-effort mode, decides and reports): undo that one too, rewrite it, or cancel. Corrections also attach to the right thing. A correction to *world truth* — which school Bobby attended — belongs to that fact and applies in whichever continuation is active. A correction to a *specific line* in a specific message belongs to that exact record, and goes dormant if that message's continuation does. The engine never guesses that a line in another continuation is "the same" one; it can suggest the match, and the author decides.

Because amendments sit off the message timeline, they **survive truncation**: delete everything after 800 and a rewrite you made at 1,001 stays in force for the surviving history (its effects on the deleted messages fall away, and come back if you undo the deletion). Undoing a rewrite is its own operation — a deliberate history edit shouldn't vanish because you trimmed later messages. A *directed regeneration* ("make the knocker George") is different: it's an ordinary narrative event at its message's position and is trimmed like any other.

For audit and debugging, the engine also keeps the **as-originally-recorded** view — what the story looked like at a checkpoint (right after reply N, or after a later in-between change) at the moment that checkpoint was recorded, before any later corrections, deletions, or summaries. It's never the default for anything player-facing.

**Undoing a rewind after you've kept playing.** Truncate to 800, write 801′ through 850′, then change your mind and undo the deletion. Rather than refusing, or wiping out the new turns, the engine **swaps**: the original 801–1,000 become the active timeline again and 801′–850′ go *dormant* — kept, and swappable back. This is regeneration's "alternatives at the same position" generalized from one message to a whole continuation: each turn has a variant, exactly one variant per position is active, and the active ones form a single chain. If you'd rather keep both side by side, a dormant continuation can be promoted into its own story through a normal fork. The one way to give any of this up is **compaction**, which a story must opt into to reclaim storage: it permanently deletes chosen dormant continuations and discarded stretches — never the active story, its rewrites, or the record of anything the game confirmed — but only after showing exactly what will go and which undos and swaps will stop being possible, and only once the game confirms — or, if the story opts into an automatic compaction policy, on that schedule, with each run recorded and reported. Anything that was *resolved* in a continuation — a lazily decided belief, a deferred knock finally answered — belongs to that continuation: swap back to the original and its own resolutions return, while anything resolved only in the dormant branch becomes unresolved again. History rewrites stay in force across a swap, and the usual conflict check flags any that no longer fit.

Whole-story deletion, right-to-be-forgotten, and opt-in compaction are the deliberate destructive operations (as opposed to reversible in-story truncation); because stories are self-contained, deletion and right-to-be-forgotten are simple scoped deletes. RTBF of a subject who appears in several forks must be applied to each — the cost of isolation, and the behavior you want.

## 14. What Epistrel enables that open roleplay cannot

Secrets, lies, and private knowledge that persist and stay private; dramatic irony and mysteries that hold up over thousands of turns; stable long games whose past does not drift or collapse; regeneration that varies only what was genuinely undecided; safe self-policing worlds (e.g., teen-appropriate roleplay) that deflect in character and resist poisoning; author retcons that reconcile the world instead of corrupting it; and integration with real games whose authoritative state lives outside the engine.

## 15. Relationship to prior art

Epistrel stands on well-established ideas and departs on one axis. **Generative Agents** (memory stream + reflection + importance) and **MemGPT/Letta** (tiered, paged memory) inform the memory tiers and consolidation. **Graphiti/Zep** pioneered bitemporal fact validity; Epistrel implements the same bitemporal model natively in Postgres and can offer a graph backend optionally. **Mem0, Cognee, LangMem** and the wider OSS memory field converged on hybrid retrieval, temporal invalidation, and consolidation as table stakes — all included. **The departure:** every one of those is built around an agent-plus-user or multi-user-ACL model. None centers a **multi-character epistemic model** — per-character belief, false belief, nested theory-of-mind, time-bound could-have-known. Research on role-playing agents (RECTOM/"Mind the Perspective," MindForge, RoleThink) identifies exactly this as the open problem. An ACL says *who may read a fact*; Epistrel models *what each character believes to be true, including what is false.* That is the difference between agent memory and character memory.

## 16. Scope and non-goals

**In scope (the product):** Epistrel = **Engine Core** (storage, epistemic model, retrieval/resolution, deterministic verification, commitment lifecycle, policy store, mutation primitives) + **Orchestrator** (the agent loop, model access, verification escalation, grading, regeneration/repair, guardrail enforcement, extensibility for external data, and background consolidation), plus the **catalog** of reusable characters and scenarios with their media, story creation from it, **content ratings** with their prompt guidance and guardrails, **images** — stored, prompted, checked, optionally generated, and placed in stories — and **engine profiles** that set how much of the engine each story uses, from Lite to Max. Plus the APIs: REST and an embeddable library. There is no MCP server; a game that wants to offer MCP access builds it over the REST API with its own sign-in and permissions.

**Not the product:** the model *processes* (external, OpenAI-compatible, configured inside Epistrel but run elsewhere — any endpoint speaking the protocol connects, but a model is called *capable* for a purpose only if it passes that purpose's conformance suite), any front-end/game, user accounts and permissions (the game signs users in and decides what each may do; Epistrel records who is acting and who owns what), and video **generation** (images can be generated through Epistrel's adapter or by the game and uploaded; videos are only uploaded). The consumer builds the game and the I/O to Epistrel; it does not build an orchestrator or any memory logic.

**Target deployment:** self-hosted at dev/hobby scale (ten stories in active play at once on one modest server — 4 cores, 16 GB of memory — measured with the model's own time set aside), with ports and boundaries designed so a future platform-scale deployment swaps adapters rather than rewriting the engine. One `docker compose up` runs everything — the engine, its database, and the background workers that own every timed cleanup — with no external scheduler or broker.

**How it gets built.** This paper and the specification describe the whole engine, but it isn't built all at once. Two groundwork milestones come first — infrastructure, then project setup and CI — and are not yet planned in detail. The engine itself is then delivered in seven milestones, each a working, tested release that keeps every guarantee delivered before it. First comes the Lite pipeline, with storage, rewind, guardrails, and ratings, so a game can integrate early. The catalog, with Lite engine profiles, comes next, then the structured memory; then per-character knowledge; the world of tracked values, the clock, and external systems; history rewriting, switching between pipelines, and the Standard, Full, and Max presets; and images last. Work on some of these overlaps, but they are released in this order. The first of these is for development only: erasure arrives in the next one, before any release that holds real players' data.

**Who may do what is the game's decision.** Epistrel is a backend component that sits behind the game, never in front of players. The game signs its users in and decides what each may do — who plays which character, who may direct or rewrite a story, who may see the all-knowing view, who may edit which catalog entries; Epistrel only checks that the caller is the game. With every change the game names who is acting, and Epistrel records it, so a multiplayer story shows who typed what. Every story, character, scenario, persona, image, and engine profile has an owner, plus any other relationships the game wants to note (*editor*, *co-author*, *player*), which Epistrel stores and lists but never interprets. When the game deletes a user, it can find everything linked to them and decide type by type — delete their stories, hand their characters and scenarios to someone else — with a warning first if, say, a story to be deleted is shared with other players. What Epistrel *does* guarantee is what a viewpoint receives: every response is produced for the character it names, the all-knowing view is returned only when explicitly requested, and a stored reply is never handed out for a different viewpoint than the one it was made for; under profiles with per-character knowledge and perception, characters' private state also comes back only in that view. That is a promise about the request, not the requester: if the game asks for the all-knowing view on a player's behalf, Epistrel can't tell. How much a player may narrate *other* characters is a per-story choice: by default their input describing another character ("Jenni hands me the key") is a request the world resolves; a co-author mode lets players narrate other characters' actions, still checked against those characters' viewpoints. Under Lite, every character's secrets share one prompt and replies aren't filtered by viewpoint, so a Lite reply can reveal a secret; the published guarantee table says so.

**What erasure covers.** Erasing a subject deletes their structured records and finds and redacts or regenerates derived prose that mentions them (reflections, summaries, rendered and rejected messages) using a reference index maintained at write time; caches are rebuilt; an erasure ledger is reapplied after any backup restore; and Epistrel's own logs never carry story content by default. Exports already handed to the game and data held by model, image, or data providers — including whatever a hosted image checker kept of the pictures and ages it was sent — are outside Epistrel's reach, and the documentation says so. Images are found through the people they're declared to show and the names in their prompts and captions — every uploaded picture must say who's in it — because Epistrel doesn't recognize faces: someone who appears in a picture only visually, unnamed and undeclared, is outside what erasure can reach. Erasure is also one of only two deliberate exceptions to "the event log is the truth" — the other is compaction, which a story must opt into (§12): it removes events, not just hides them, so afterwards the log, replays, forks, and restored backups all reflect the story *without* the erased person, and the history as it was before can't be rebuilt. Audit views and backup checks show a marker where something was erased — never the content — so a gap reads as an erasure, not as corruption.
